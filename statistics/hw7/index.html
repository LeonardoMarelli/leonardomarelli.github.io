Statistical Independence: Contingency Table and χ² Test

Contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts under independence, χ² statistic and p-value.

1. Problem and method

Given a bivariate dataset of pairs (xk, fk), k = 1..N, we ask whether the two variables are independent. The program works in these steps:

  1. Contingency table: nij = number of pairs with X = xi and F = fj.
  2. Marginals: ni· = Σj nij, n·j = Σi nij, N = Σ nij. Relative frequencies: fij = nij/N, fi· = ni·/N, f·j = n·j/N.
  3. Conditional frequencies: f(F=j | X=i) = nij/ni· and f(X=i | F=j) = nij/n·j.
  4. Expected counts under independence: Eij = ni· n·j / N.
  5. Statistic: χ² = Σij (nij − Eij)² / Eij, with df = (r−1)(c−1).
  6. p-value (optional part): P(χ²df ≥ observed) = Q(df/2, χ²/2), where Q is the regularised upper incomplete gamma function. It is implemented from scratch (series and continued fraction), with no libraries.
  7. Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N · (min(r,c) − 1))), an effect size between 0 and 1. The p-value alone depends on N: with a large sample even a tiny association gives a small p-value.

2. Independence: theory

  • Probability: X and F are independent iff P(X=x, F=f) = P(X=x) P(F=f) for all x, f. Equivalently, P(F=f | X=x) = P(F=f): knowing X does not change the distribution of F.
  • Statistics: the empirical counterpart is fij = fi· f·j, i.e. nij = Eij, and all conditional distributions equal the marginal one. Perfect equality never happens in a finite sample, because of random fluctuation.
  • Test: H0: X and F are independent. Under H0 the statistic χ² is approximately χ²(r−1)(c−1) for large N. The approximation is considered acceptable when all Eij ≥ 5. A small p-value means the observed table would be unlikely if H0 were true.

3. Datasets and results

Two datasets are used. Hair and eye colour is real data: 592 students classified by hair colour (black, brown, red, blond) and eye colour (brown, blue, hazel, green), the counts of R's built-in HairEyeColor table summed over sex. Simulated data (N = 600: operating system vs breach) are drawn with seed 7 from two independent distributions, so independence holds by construction and the test can be checked on a known case.

The tables are generated when you click Run.

4. Interpretation

Hair and eye colour: dependence

  • χ² ≈ 138.3 with df = 9 gives p ≈ 10−25: we reject independence. Hair colour and eye colour are statistically dependent.
  • The conditional frequencies show how. Among blond students 74% have blue eyes, against 36% overall; among black-haired students 63% have brown eyes, against 37% overall. Under independence every row would equal the marginal row (37% brown, 36% blue, 16% hazel, 11% green).
  • The largest contributions to χ² come from blond with blue eyes (94 observed, about 46 expected) and blond with brown eyes (7 observed, about 47 expected), then black hair with brown eyes (68 observed, about 40 expected).
  • Cramér's V ≈ 0.28 indicates a moderate association. All Eij are at least 7.7, so the χ² approximation is acceptable.

Simulated: compatible with independence

  • χ² ≈ 5.84 with df = 2 gives p ≈ 0.054, just above 0.05: at α = 0.05 we do not reject independence. The conditional frequencies are close to the marginal ones, and Cramér's V ≈ 0.10 is small.
  • This is a borderline result, and it shows the logic of the test: even when independence is true by construction, random sampling produces some deviation, and about 5% of such samples fall below p = 0.05. Failing to reject is lack of evidence against independence, not proof of it.

Association is not causation

  • The χ² test only says that the variables are statistically associated. It says nothing about why.
  • Hair colour does not cause eye colour, nor the reverse. The association is plausibly due to common background factors, such as ancestry and the genes that control pigmentation, which affect both traits. This is an example of a common cause (confounding).
  • The sample is also a particular group of students, so the result describes that group. It does not automatically extend to other populations.
  • The association is symmetric: the test treats X and F the same way, so it gives no direction of influence. Establishing causality requires a randomised experiment or an explicit causal model, plus control of confounders.
