Caesar Cipher, Toy Elliptic-Curve Scheme and Key-Space Attacks

Encrypt and decrypt with a Caesar cipher and with a toy elliptic-curve scheme, attack both, and compare the statistics and the key spaces.

2. Caesar cipher: encryption and decryption

The Caesar cipher replaces each letter by the letter a fixed number of places further along the alphabet, wrapping around after Z. With letters numbered A = 0 to Z = 25 and key k:

C = (P + k) mod 26 (encryption) and P = (C − k) mod 26 (decryption).

3. Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher


        
Method

        

            Shifting does not change the shape of the letter distribution, only its position. The attack uses a
            reference distribution fi of the language and works in three steps:
        

        
    
            
  1. Count the letters of the ciphertext.
    2. 
            
  2. For each of the 26 possible keys (including 0), decrypt and compare the letter counts with the reference
                using the chi-square statistic:
                χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected, with expected = total × fi.
    3. 
            
  3. Choose the key with the smallest chi-square.
    4. 
        

        

            The 5% critical value for 25 degrees of freedom is 37.65. The absolute value grows with the length of the text,
            so the candidates are best compared with each other.

Effectiveness and limitations

        
    
            
  • Short texts: with a few letters the observed counts are noisy, and the correct shift often
                does not have the smallest chi-square. The key is then recovered only sometimes.
    • 
            
  • Longer texts: the counts approach the reference distribution, so the correct key becomes
                the clear minimum. The success rate rises toward 100% and the mean rank of the true key falls to 1.
    • 
            
  • Limits of the method: the test assumes that the plaintext is ordinary prose in the chosen language.
                Names, numbers, codes, or text in another language, or a short message that is unusually rich in rare letters,
                all break it. The statistic also needs a reliable reference distribution; the tables used here differ by about a percent between sources.
    • 
            
  • Limits of the experiment: the corpus is only about 600 letters, so windows for long lengths overlap heavily.
                The measured rates are therefore not independent estimates. A larger corpus would give more reliable numbers.
    • 
        

    


    
    

        
5. Alternative approaches (optional)

        
    
            
  • Exhaustive key search: decrypt with all 26 keys and read the results. It always works, but the final choice
                still needs a judgment or a statistic. With 25 useful keys it costs almost nothing.
    • 
            
  • Letter-frequency analysis (chi-square): the method of section 4. It uses the whole distribution.
    • 
            
  • Log-likelihood: the score Σ counti · ln(fi) is the log of the probability of the observed counts under the reference model. It
                is maximised by the same key as chi-square in most cases, because for large samples the two statistics are closely related.
                The sections above show both columns.
    • 
            
  • N-gram scoring: instead of single letters, score pairs or triples of letters (for example with English bigram tables).
                This uses more of the structure of the language, so it is more powerful for short texts. It was not implemented here, because a reliable bigram table
                needs a large corpus, and the only corpus available is the 600-letter sample that is also used for testing. Estimating the table from the test text itself would be a form of data leakage.
    • 
        

    


    
    

        
6. The toy elliptic-curve scheme (Part 1 recap)

        

            The toy scheme uses the curve y² = x³ + 7, which has the same shape as secp256k1 (a = 0, b = 7),
            but over the small prime field F1019. The real secp256k1 uses a 256-bit prime. Because
            1019 ≡ 2 (mod 3), the curve has 1019 affine points plus the point at infinity O. The subgroup generated by the base point G
            has a much smaller order n, shown in the table.
        


        
Main steps and role of the keys

        
    
            
  1. Public parameters: the curve, the base point G and its order n. Everybody knows these.
    2. 
            
  2. Key generation: the owner picks a private key d at random in the restricted range 1..KEY_MAX, and publishes Q = dG.
                d stays secret. Q is public. Recovering d from Q is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem.
    3. 
            
  3. Encoding: each symbol i (A..Z and space, so 27 symbols) is mapped to the point Mi = (i+1)G.
    4. 
            
  4. Encryption: for each symbol the sender picks a fresh random r, and sends C1 = rG and C2 = Mi + rQ.
                r is never sent. Using a new r for each symbol makes equal symbols produce different ciphertexts.
    5. 
            
  5. Decryption: the owner computes S = d·C1 = drG = rQ, then M = C2 − S, and finds the symbol whose point is M.
    6. 
        

        

            The private key d is what turns the public point C1 into the shared point rQ. Anyone who knows d
            can decrypt. Anyone who does not know d would have to compute rQ from C1 = rG and Q, which is a Diffie–Hellman problem on the curve.
        


        

            

            

            

            

            

        

    


    
    

        
7. Attack on the restricted key space

        

            The owner restricts d to a small range, which keeps the experiment computationally feasible. The attacker knows
            G, n, the curve, Q and the blocks (C1, C2), but not d or r.
            Two attacks are shown below:
        

        
    
            
  • Exhaustive search: compute G, 2G, 3G, … and stop when kG = Q. At most KEY_MAX steps.
    • 
            
  • Baby-step giant-step: write d = i·m + j with m = ⌈√(range)⌉. Store the baby steps jG,
                then walk Q − i·mG and look for a match. About 2√(range) operations instead of range.
    • 
        

        

            Once d is recovered, decryption is immediate: M = C2 − d·C1.
        

        

            

            

        


        
Difference from the Caesar attack

        

            The Caesar attack uses statistical information: the ciphertext keeps the letter distribution of the language, and
            the attack compares distributions. It returns a likely key with a degree of confidence, and it works best with more text.
        

        

            The toy attack uses no statistics at all. Each candidate key is checked exactly, with kG = Q?. The result is either correct
            or not, with certainty. What makes it possible is the size of the key space, not any property of the output. The ciphertext
            is not analysed at all: once d is known, the decryption is exact.
        

    


    
    

        
8. Why this approach fails against a properly configured ECC scheme

        

            Exhaustive search takes time proportional to the size of the key space. A properly configured scheme uses a private key of about 256 bits,
            so the key space has about 2256 elements.
        

        
    
            
  • Exhaustive search needs up to 2256 group operations.
    • 
            
  • Baby-step giant-step needs about 2128 operations, and about as much memory to store the table. This is the bottleneck.
    • 
            
  • Pollard's rho needs about 2128 operations but almost no memory. It is the best known generic method for prime-order groups of this size.
    • 
        

        

            The table below uses a rate of 1012 group operations per second as an assumption. The conclusion does not depend on the exact rate.
        

        

        

            The toy attack works because the key range is small enough to enumerate. The method does not scale: each extra bit doubles the work.
            The ECC attacks that are actually known require exponential time in the key length, which is why the ECDLP is considered intractable for well-chosen curves
            with prime-order subgroups of about 2256 elements.
        

    


    
    

        
9. Weaknesses of the toy scheme (optional)


        
Weaknesses that come from the restricted key space

        
    
            
  • Exhaustive search and baby-step giant-step recover d in a few thousand operations.
    • 
            
  • The group itself is small: the curve has only 1020 points, so the discrete logarithm is easy whatever range d is drawn from.
                Restricting the range is not the only problem; the group order is too small in the first place.
    • 
        


        
Weaknesses that come from the design of the toy scheme

        
    
            
  • No integrity: without a tag, anyone can change C2 to C2 + jG, and the receiver decrypts the symbol with index i + j.
                The attacker does not need the key. This is malleability, and it works like a Caesar shift applied to the plaintext symbols.
    • 
            
  • Reused randomness: if the same r is used for two symbols, C1 is the same, and C21 − C22 = M1 − M2
                reveals the difference of the two symbols. Good randomness for each block is required.
    • 
            
  • Small plaintext space: only 27 points are used as messages. Anyone who can test a guess about a block can check the 27 possibilities.
                This matters only together with another weakness (for example, a known or reused r), but it shows that encoding each symbol as a point is not a secure encoding.
    • 
            
  • No key derivation or symmetric encryption: each symbol is encrypted with its own group element. Practical schemes (such as ECIES, section 11)
                derive a symmetric key and use an authenticated cipher.
    •

11. Similarities and differences: Caesar cipher and elliptic-curve scheme


        
Direct comparison: Caesar vs the toy scheme (measured)

        

            The table encrypts the same English letters with Caesar (key 7) and with the toy scheme from section 7, then applies the statistical tests.
            For the toy scheme the attacker's view is the x-coordinate of each C2 reduced mod 26 (used as a letter), and the coordinates of C2 in 16 equal bins of the field.
        

        

        

            Expected reading: the Caesar ciphertext keeps the language, so the letter test recovers the key. The toy ciphertext uses a fresh r for every letter,
            so its letters should not match the language, and the coordinates should be consistent with uniform values. If the table shows otherwise,
            the text of this section needs to be corrected to match the numbers.
        

        
    
            
  • Encryption: Caesar uses one secret shift and no randomness. The toy scheme uses the public key Q and a fresh random r per letter.
    • 
            
  • Decryption: Caesar subtracts k from each letter. The toy scheme needs the private key d, two scalar multiplications per letter, and a lookup table of 27 points.
    • 
            
  • Key space: 25 keys for Caesar; about 210 private keys for the toy scheme (section 8). Both are small, so both fall to exhaustive search.
    • 
            
  • Statistics: Caesar leaks the language. The toy scheme does not leak it through the letter test. Its weaknesses are structural (sections 8 and 10), not statistical.
    • 
        


        
Encryption and decryption

        
    
            
  • Similarity: both schemes are reversible by the holder of a secret, and both apply a simple arithmetic operation
                (addition of a shift, or addition of points on the curve).
    • 
            
  • Difference: the Caesar cipher uses one key for both directions and acts on each letter independently with a fixed transformation.
                The toy scheme uses a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption, and each block depends on a fresh random r.
    • 
        


        
Key spaces

        
    
            
  • Caesar: 25 useful keys. Exhaustive search is trivial.
    • 
            
  • Toy scheme: KEY_MAX keys, which is about 210. Exhaustive search is feasible in milliseconds.
    • 
            
  • Real ECC: about 2256 keys. Exhaustive search is infeasible, and the best known generic attacks still need about 2128 operations.
    • 
        


        
Statistical properties

        
    
            
  • Caesar: the ciphertext keeps the letter distribution of the language. The statistics reveal the scheme, and the key follows from them.
    • 
            
  • Modern scheme: the ciphertext bytes are close to uniform, and the letter attack finds no structure. Statistics cannot reveal the key,
                but they also cannot prove security: a scheme can look random and still be broken by a protocol error.
    • 
        

    


    
    

        
12. Conclusion

        

            The Caesar cipher is broken by a letter-frequency test, with or without a known key space, and it works well only for long texts.
            The toy elliptic-curve scheme is correct as a mathematical construction, but its key space and its group are too small to be secure.
            Attacking it is an exhaustive search, not a statistical one. The statistical and key-space arguments show why the parameters of a real
            scheme must be large, and why integrity and fresh randomness are also required.
