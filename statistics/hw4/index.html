Penetration Score: LLN and CLT

We model the score accumulated while an attacker probes a sequence of systems. By simulating many independent attackers, we can compare what the theory predicts with what happens in finite samples.

1. Model

Let Xi be the score contribution at step i, and assume the increments are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). The total score after n steps is Sn = ∑i=1n Xi. Each simulated trajectory represents one attacker across n systems; the parameter m is the number of independent trajectories used to estimate the distributions.

  • Bernoulli: Xi ∈ {0, 1}, with P(Xi = 1) = p. Its mean is μ = p and its variance is σ² = p(1 − p).
  • Rademacher: Xi ∈ {−1, +1}, each with probability 1/2. Its mean is μ = 0 and its variance is σ² = 1, so positive and negative increments balance on average.
  • Quantities studied: the total Sn, the average Sn/n, and the scaled total Sn/√n. These use different scales to show how the score behaves as the number of systems grows.

This is a simplified statistical model: the increments represent abstract score changes, not a realistic simulation of the technical steps of a cyberattack.

2. Simulation

For each selected value of n = 10, 100, 1000, 10000, the program generates m independent scores. It reports sample means and variances, with theoretical reference columns for most quantities. For the variance of the scaled total, compare the empirical value of Var(Sn/√n) with its theoretical value σ² (1 for Rademacher, p(1 − p) for Bernoulli). Agreement is expected to improve with more samples, although random variation remains.

How to read the results: columns marked “th.” are theoretical values; the other columns are calculated from the simulated sample. The estimated probability P(|Sn/n − μ| > 0.05) measures how often the sample average differs from its expected value by more than 0.05. Chebyshev's bound, min(1, σ²/(nε²)), is a theoretical upper bound on that probability, with ε = 0.05; it need not be tight.

The histogram compares the standardised total Zn = (Sn − nμ)/√(nσ²) with the standard normal distribution N(0, 1). The KS value measures the largest difference between their cumulative distribution functions. For Rademacher increments, μ = 0 and σ² = 1, so Zn = Sn/√n.

3. Aggregate over attackers

Here we fix n = 100 systems per attacker and calculate A = ∑j=1m Sn(j), the combined score of all m attackers. Since the trajectories are independent, E[A] = mnμ and Var(A) = mnσ². Dividing by mn gives the average score per attacker-step, whose variance is σ²/(mn). This lets us see how adding attackers affects the aggregate and its average.

4. What the numbers show

Law of large numbers (LLN)

  • The average Sn/n concentrates around μ because its variance is σ²/n, which tends to zero.
  • For any fixed ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) tends to zero. This is convergence in probability; Chebyshev's inequality gives an upper bound for the probability of a deviation.

Variance under each normalisation

  • Var(Sn) = nσ²: the total's variance increases with the number of steps.
  • Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n: the average becomes more concentrated.
  • Var(Sn/√n) = σ²: scaling by √n keeps the variance constant.
  • For Bernoulli increments, E[Sn/√n] = p√n, so this uncentered quantity does not approach N(0, 1). The centered and standardised sum is the appropriate quantity for the CLT.

Central limit theorem (CLT)

  • The CLT says that (Sn − nμ)/√(nσ²) converges in distribution to N(0, 1) as n grows, under the assumptions used here.
  • In the Rademacher model, μ = 0 and σ² = 1, so this becomes exactly Sn/√n → N(0, 1), as requested. The histogram and KS statistic give a numerical check; with finite m, the empirical KS value fluctuates and need not decrease at every step.

Systems and attackers

  • As n increases, the average score becomes more predictable in both models. In the Bernoulli model the total has mean np; in the Rademacher model its mean stays zero, while its standard deviation grows like √n.
  • For fixed n, adding independent attackers multiplies the aggregate mean by m and its variance by m. The average over all mn attacker-steps has variance σ²/(mn), so it becomes more concentrated as attackers are added.
  • A single attacker's raw total can remain variable even when n is large; the law of large numbers guarantees stability of the per-step average, not of every rescaled quantity.
